# Editorial reassessment: lessons 1–300

Reviewed on 9 October 2026 from the perspective of teaching English to Sinhala-speaking Sri Lankan learners.

## Judgement

The reviewed range is suitable for an initial **supported beginner course** after the corrections in this pass. Its practical vocabulary, Sinhala support, question practice and local situations provide a useful foundation. It should not be described as proof that completing 300 lessons establishes A2 proficiency or independent fluency.

This is a source-content and software review, not accreditation, an independent human-professor sign-off, or a learner trial. Audio clips were not listened to during this review.

## Scope and method

Reviewed the assembled examples and patterns across all 30 modules, the underlying bilingual authoring files, corrections, vocabulary, practice tasks and the app's quiz/display behaviour. Checked crucial grammar distinctions against primary teaching references. Automated checks cover completeness, IDs, source-linked quizzes, encoding and specific editorial regressions; they cannot establish the naturalness of every translation or the effectiveness of instruction.

The 300 lessons contain 1,500 bilingual model-sentence slots, 1,200 vocabulary slots and 300 guided-practice tasks. Reusing language across later modules is useful revision; each numbered lesson should nevertheless have a distinct teaching purpose.

## Findings and changes made

| Finding | Evidence | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early examples sometimes outran their teaching notes | Lesson 2 mixed `How are you?` with present perfect and past questions; lessons 24–40 mixed basic sentence order/articles with unrelated past actions | Simplified targeted examples to the taught present-time patterns and updated affected vocabulary |
| Another-person introduction was not consistently modelled | Lesson 8 included `Have you met …?` and a past sentence under `This is …` | All five models now demonstrate `This is …` |
| A countability correction rejected a valid serving request | Lesson 125 treated `a lime juice` as an error | Explain material versus serving meanings; the correction now addresses the genuine singular/plural error `a glasses` |
| Question-word guidance overstated a preference | Lesson 84 rejected `What bus …?` merely because options were given | Removed that misleading correction and acknowledge contextual overlap between `what` and `which` |
| Spoken number words were mistaken for an error | Lesson 98 contrasted a written digit with the spoken word `one` | Use a real agreement error and retain number-confirmation practice |
| A useful exception was missing | Lesson 36 used `some` in an offer but its rule concentrated on `any` in questions | Explain `some` in offers and requests |
| Possessive examples exceeded the explanation | Lesson 55 included `parents’` and `children’s` but only explained singular `’s` | Explain singular, regular plural and irregular plural possession |
| Some examples teach appropriateness rather than grammar | Greetings, distance with `this/that`, frequency-adverb placement and declarative confirmation questions | Mark these corrections as contextual; show the situation and avoid crossing out valid English as universally wrong |
| Context-sensitive quiz questions did not state the situation | Choosing an appropriate greeting or connector requires context | Include the explicit situation in the correction-question prompt |
| Some later vocabulary was less tightly connected to the models | Lesson 228 taught a past story using `woke`, `ran`, `sat`, `told`, while its vocabulary listed other verbs | Align the vocabulary and correction; also tighten lessons 227 and 230 |
| One Sinhala module name confused pronouns with nouns | “Pronouns & SVO Order” used the Sinhala term for nouns | Change it to `සර්වනාම` |

The changes preserve lesson numbers and phrase IDs. Lessons 301–1000 are outside this review. The production lesson deck continues to use the reviewed bundled content for lessons 1–300 even when an older database snapshot is loaded.

## Module-level assessment

| Lessons | Teaching area | Assessment and teaching emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–10 | Greetings and introductions | Improved difficulty control. Learn polite expressions as meaningful chunks, then use them with different people. |
| 11–20 | Personal information | Useful self-introduction progression. Practise real follow-up questions; learners may invent personal details. |
| 21–30 | Pronouns and sentence order | Strong correction of Sinhala-to-English word-order transfer after removing unnecessary tense changes. |
| 31–40 | Nouns and articles | Sound-based a/an and context-based the are sound priorities. Explain some/any exceptions using the actual offer example. |
| 41–50 | Demonstratives | Use physical objects or pictures to make distance and number observable. This/that choices are contextual. |
| 51–60 | Possession and family | Distinguish my/mine, singular/plural possession and family relationships. Accept an invented family for practice. |
| 61–70 | Present simple routines | Good repetition of routines, agreement and frequency. Personalise the timetable rather than memorise model details. |
| 71–80 | Negatives and short answers | Useful separation of don't/doesn't and be negatives. Repeat answers from the perspective of the person responding. |
| 81–90 | Basic questions | Good range of question purposes. Standard question order is the instructional target, while confirmation questions can be valid speech. |
| 91–100 | Time, dates and numbers | Practical information-checking tasks. Written dates and spoken ordinals should be practised together. |
| 101–110 | Family descriptions | Broadens the earlier family unit with appearance, personality and roles. Add detail without requiring every family to fit the examples. |
| 111–120 | Home and objects | Concrete objects support there is/are and location phrases. Ask learners to describe their own room or a picture. |
| 121–130 | Food and drink | Strong local relevance. Teach quantities, preferences and requests; retain valid serving expressions and local dish names. |
| 131–140 | Shopping | Functional requests and comparison. Prices are fictional examples; return/refund questions ask about shop policy rather than promise a right. |
| 141–150 | Directions | Good progression from asking to confirming. Use a simple map; knowing the sentence alone is not evidence of understanding the route. |
| 151–160 | Classroom communication | Particularly useful for asking for help and clarification. Use these expressions during subsequent lessons. |
| 161–170 | Work and jobs | Suitable familiar work exchanges. Job roles do not require gender stereotypes; practise a first-day conversation. |
| 171–180 | Health communication | Language for describing symptoms and requesting professional help. Some past/perfect expressions are supported functional chunks before their later grammar units. |
| 181–190 | Weather and plans | Useful introductory plan language. Treat `if it rains` as a supported chunk here; formal conditional study belongs later. |
| 191–200 | Everyday review | Good integrated practice. Change names, prices and locations so the learner must transfer the language. |
| 201–210 | Past be | Clear state/location focus and a useful contrast with actions. Briefly scaffold the action forms previewed before lesson 211. |
| 211–220 | Regular past verbs | Teach spelling and sound separately: an -ed spelling does not always add a syllable. |
| 221–230 | Irregular past verbs | Good recurring stories. Keep vocabulary and corrections tied to the verbs learners actually see. |
| 231–240 | Past questions and stories | Subject questions, follow-ups, sequencing and clarification make this more than tense drilling. |
| 241–250 | Past continuous | Explicitly show be + -ing before contrasting background and interrupting events. Present continuous appears later, so do not assume it is already mastered. |
| 251–260 | Will | Decisions, offers, promises and predictions are usefully separated. Avoid treating will as the only possible future form. |
| 261–270 | Going to | The prior-plan/current-decision distinction is a useful teaching simplification; real usage can overlap. |
| 271–280 | Present continuous | Good now/temporary/routine contrasts. State verbs and future arrangements add difficulty and benefit from teacher support. |
| 281–290 | Present perfect | Useful experience/result distinction, but a noticeable difficulty increase. Treat this as supported extension material, not evidence of a completed CEFR level. |
| 291–300 | Comparisons and connected speech | Good progression into reasons and choices. Equal comparison, although and the final mixed-tense challenge require slower, supported practice. |

## Remaining material limitations

1. **The quiz primarily tests recognition and reconstruction.** It includes translation-choice, word order, gap selection and a recommended correction. A high score does not establish independent speaking, listening comprehension or flexible grammar use.
2. **Speaking remains optional and audio is model playback.** The written tasks are helpful, but this review did not verify speech recognition, accent feedback or the pronunciation of Sri Lankan names and dishes by the actual voice.
3. **Difficulty is not uniform within the A1/A2 navigation labels.** Polite expressions, health-duration language and later perfect/connector work need scaffolding. CEFR is based on communicative performance, not a list of grammar structures or a lesson count.
4. **Recycling should involve retrieval.** At module reviews, ask about a previous topic with different facts. Do not count reciting the displayed five sentences as independent fluency.
5. **Old pending quiz attempts need release awareness.** Quiz content is generated from the current bundle while saved attempts contain lesson/version/seed and answers. Stable IDs preserve navigation and progress; they do not guarantee identical grading for attempts created before a content change. Content-version migration was not implemented in this editorial pass.

## Suggested pilot assessment

At lessons 100, 200 and 300, hide the model text and ask learners to perform the existing speaking task with changed details. Observe these four things:

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Task completion | Gives the necessary information or completes the request/plan |
| Interaction | Answers an unprepared follow-up and asks for clarification when needed |
| Intelligibility | The partner can identify the important name, amount, time or event |
| Language control | Uses the lesson's core pattern sufficiently clearly to convey the intended meaning |

For listening, play or say a short exchange using the reviewed vocabulary and ask learners to identify a time, price, destination or event without the written model. This is a suggested teacher-led pilot procedure; no new proficiency scoring system was added to the app.

## Reference basis

- [Council of Europe self-assessment grid](https://rm.coe.int/16802fc1bf): A1 supported interaction and A2 familiar routine exchanges inform the teaching judgement.
- [Cambridge English Grammar Today: some](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/some): some can occur in questions, including expected-positive contexts.
- [Cambridge Dictionary: tea](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/english/tea?q=Tea): drinks can have countable serving uses.
- [Cambridge English Grammar Today: which](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/which): question-word choice depends on the range of possible answers.
- [British Council: present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-perfect): present connection and finished-past-time contrasts support lessons 281–290.

## Verification

Regression tests cover foundation simplification, article/possession exceptions, contextual quiz situations, bilingual structure and preservation of source IDs. Existing foundation, everyday, grammar and all-course quiz tests are rerun alongside TypeScript and the production build. The full 1,000-lesson publication audit still has failures in the unreviewed later course; those are not evidence that lessons 1–300 were fully quality-assured by that audit.
